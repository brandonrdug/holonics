## 1. Why `hex` is a productive coincidence, not the etymology

The English verb/noun `hex`, meaning witchcraft or a curse, comes through Pennsylvania German
`hexe` and German `hexen` / `Hexe`. Geometric `hexa-` comes from Greek `hex`, six. `Hexagon` is
Greek `hexa-` plus `gonia`, angle. `Hexadecimal` names sixteen through the conventional mixed
formation six-plus-ten; it is not named after a six-sided shape.

The homonym can still be conceptually productive. It simply cannot be used as historical evidence
that culture named curses after a fundamental geometric face.

## 2. The exact cube/hexagon relation

For a `d`-dimensional axis-aligned rectangular cell:

```text
codimension-one boundary faces = 2d
vertices                         = 2^d.
```

Therefore:

```text
d=2: square cell -> 4 boundary edges, 4 vertices
d=3: cube cell   -> 6 boundary faces, 8 vertices.
```

This is the exact `2d` versus `2^d` relation already present in the record. At `d=2` the counts
coincide; at `d=3` the interior has more corners than boundary faces. The distinction begins at
three axes.

A cube viewed orthogonally along a body diagonal has a hexagonal silhouette. Other orientations
give rectangular, square, or general hexagonal projected hulls. Formally, for viewing direction
`u`, the observer receives a projection

```text
Q_u : R^3 -> u^perp.
```

`Q_u` is not injective: depth along `u` is collapsed. Infinitely many different three-dimensional
bodies can cast the same two-dimensional face. Changing `u` changes the face without changing the
body; changing the body can preserve the face. This is a clean geometric model of testimony versus
interior.

The cube is not the unique simplest volume. A tetrahedron is the three-dimensional simplex and has
four triangular faces and four vertices; a sphere has no planar polygonal faces. Six is therefore
fundamental only after the rectangular three-axis frame has been declared.

### The counts must retain their grain

Take the cube vertices as `(plus_or_minus 1, plus_or_minus 1, plus_or_minus 1)` and project along
`u=(1,1,1)/sqrt(3)`. The two body-diagonal vertices project to the same central image coordinate,
while the other six become the regular hexagonal hull. Thus one exact projection carries:

```text
8 source vertices -> 7 image coordinates -> 6 hull corners.
```

None of those counts is wrong; each counts a different typed population.

An ordinary regular planar hexagram is different again. It is the compound star polygon `{6/2}`:
two equilateral three-cycles. Its two triangle boundaries have six finite mutual edge crossings,
not eight. Subdividing the drawn segments at those crossings gives six outer tips plus six crossing
vertices. The connected planar graph then has `V=12`, `E=18`, and therefore `F=8` regions including
the exterior. There really is an eight in this face, but it counts complementary regions rather
than triangle-triangle intersections. If the triangle sides are extended as projective lines, the
three parallel direction-pairs meet at infinity and the full line-pair incidence count becomes
nine. The population changes with the declared body, completion, and grain.

This is the correction that preserves Brandon's stronger point: **what the receiver is solving is
the incidence family compatible with the face, not the face in isolation.**

## 5. Color wheels are charts over an observer/instrument relation

Hexadecimal color notation is a coordinate encoding, not a color ontology. Six-digit CSS
`#RRGGBB` gives three 8-bit sRGB channel coordinates, so its declared grid contains

```text
256^3 = 2^24
```

possible channel triples. The underlying ideal sRGB cube uses coordinates in `[0,1]^3`; the six
hexadecimal digits are one finite grain over that cube.

A color wheel is another chart. HSL holds a cylindrical hue angle beside saturation and lightness;
other wheels and color spaces choose different axes, white points, gamuts, and perceptual metrics.
The wheel is not the infinity of color. It is a useful local parameterization of distinctions under
a declared observer and viewing condition.

Thus `green` is neither an intrinsic label attached to one wavelength nor an arbitrary fiction.
It is a relatively stable category constrained by receptor physiology, illumination, adaptation,
surrounding faces, language history, and task. One can interpolate indefinitely in an ideal
continuous color space, while an 8-bit display and a named category impose different discrete
partitions. Even the interpolation path depends on whether it occurs in gamma-encoded sRGB,
linear-light sRGB, HSL, Lab, or another chart.

Write this generally as:

```text

```

where `F` is frame, `g` grain, and `O` the contemporary objective. A discrete class is the fiber of
that map. It is real for the declared use without becoming a globally natural kind.

## 6. The two guards: one communication event can close the invariant

The standard puzzle assumes:

- exactly two doors and exactly one safe door;
- exactly one truthful guard and one lying guard;
- both know the safe door, each other's type, the language, and the question;
- answers are deterministic and restricted to the declared door relation.

Let the safe-door proposition be the bit `s`. Truth acts as `T(x)=x`; lying acts as
`L(x)=not x`. Ask either guard:

```text
Which door would the other guard say is safe?
```

If the respondent is truthful, they accurately report the liar's `not s`. If the respondent is the
liar, they negate the truthful guard's `s`, again returning `not s`. Therefore the composed answer
is invariant to which guard was selected:

```text
answer = not s;
choose not(answer) = s.
```

Only one question to one guard is required in the classic form. Asking both is required only by
changed variants whose assumptions remove the composition above.

The important holonic result survives Brandon's point. The observer need not identify either guard
or possess a stored answer. The problem statement supplies relative invariants; one communication
event composes them into a new invariant at the decision boundary. If either guard may be random,
ignorant, role-changing, or unrestricted in response, the one-question construction fails. The
solution belongs to the stated ecosystem and no larger one.

This is a precise small face of localized solving: a global search over hidden identities collapses
to one local deed after the relational symmetry is carried correctly. It is not a statement about
the classical `P` versus `NP` problem.

## 7. Unicursal order, Penrose closure, the lemniscate, and the gyroparallelogram

`Unicursal` means that the complete graph can be traversed in one route. The ordinary regular

the edge word—one standard ordering is `0 -> 3 -> 5 -> 1 -> 4 -> 2 -> 0`—so all six outer vertices
belong to one circuit. A pentagram `{5/2}` is already one circuit because `gcd(5,2)=1`.

This is why a unicursal hexagram can resemble a pentagram without becoming one. Their common face
is a one-component star traversal; their number of vertices, step word, crossings, angles, and
incidence graph remain different. Most importantly, the same six boundary points support either
two closed triangles or one closed circuit. **Adjacency and order, not the point palette, determine
the carried path.**
